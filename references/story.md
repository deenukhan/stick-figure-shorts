## 1. Why the format works
- **"You" is the hero.** Second person, present tense ("You find…", "You just…") puts the viewer inside the story from word one.
- **Stakes in line 1.** A concrete object, number or danger: "$100", "a key", "20 years", "one hour of oxygen".
- **A true fact inside a story** gets shared: people learn something and feel smart passing it on.
- **A question at the end** makes people comment; a cliffhanger makes them follow for part 2.
- **The look is cheap and consistent:** the same 2–3 characters can carry a daily series.

## 2. Premise formulas (fill in the blanks)
| Formula | Example |
|---|---|
| You find ___. Nobody saw. (what do people really do?) | A wallet with $100 → the lost-wallet experiment |
| You have ___ seconds/minutes to ___. | 60 seconds of air in a sinking car → what actually works |
| You're offered ___ or ___. | $1 million now or a penny doubled for 30 days → compounding |
| Everyone thinks ___. Then ___. | Everyone thinks the Great Wall is visible from space → it isn't |
| You wake up as ___ in ___. | A Roman soldier → what a day's pay could buy |
| You and ___ are ___. Same offer. | Two suspects, two rooms → the prisoner's dilemma |
| One small ___ changed ___. | A misplaced decimal → a real engineering failure |

Rules:
- One premise per film. If it needs a second fact, it's two films.
- The twist must be **surprising and true**. A twist that's only surprising is clickbait; one that's only true is a lecture.
- The ending is either a **question** ("Would you…?") or a **turn** ("…and that's why ___"). Never both.

## 3. Niches that fit
Money and finance facts · law and "is this legal?" · psychology experiments and dilemmas · history "a day in the life" · science "what if" · survival · business case studies · kids' fables and morals · language and word origins · health myths (with care and primary sources).

## 4. Series and audience votes
- **Part structure:** end on a binary choice ("A or B?"), make part 2 from the majority, open it with the result ("61% of you said…"). Pin part 1.
- **Use a different keyword per choice** than the page's lead-magnet keyword, so DM automations don't clash.
- **Recurring cast:** keep costume colours and hair details fixed across parts; reuse `Figure` props.
- **Daily rhythm:** one premise → one 30 s film. The fixed kit means a new film is mostly new story, new sets and new cards.

## 5. Fact rules
1. Find the **primary source** (the paper, the law, the official dataset) before writing the line. Note author, year and the exact numbers.
2. **Say what was measured.** If a study measured "contacted the owner", the film says "tried to give it back", not "returned the money".
3. **Round honestly** ("17,303" → "seventeen thousand" is fine; "about 20,000" is not).
4. If the story itself is fiction (a $100 bill), never imply the study used it.
5. Put the citation on the fact card (small mono line) or at least in `BRIEF.md`.
6. No made-up statistics, view counts or income figures, ever.

## 6. Script checklist
- [ ] 55–95 words, 20–40 s at a natural pace (~2.5 words per second plus pauses).
- [ ] Line 1 = hook. One problem. One twist. One payoff. One question.
- [ ] Sentences of 3–9 words; "..." for the pauses that matter.
- [ ] Every number checked against its source.
- [ ] Read it aloud once: anything you stumble on, ElevenLabs will too.
